On What the Job Is

The Opening

Good morning. This is the first one. There will be fifty-one more like it this year, one every Sunday, without fail. If you're reading this, you work in medicine, or you're on your way into it, or you love someone who does. You already know the job is hard. You don't need another email reminding you of that. This one won't. What we'll do instead, every week, is five minutes of the things worth paying attention to. The research, the tools, the people, and the stories from our colleagues across the field.

— Troy, Ray, & Ibrahim

From the Field
EMS · rural call, 2 a.m.

Got the call for chest pain, patient in her 70s, 35 minutes from the nearest hospital. Her husband met us at the door with a list of her medications he'd been writing out for her every week for forty-two years. When we loaded her, he grabbed my partner's arm and said, please, that's all I have. She made it. I think about that list of meds all the time.

ICU nurse · teaching hospital

A resident who started in July asked me last week, very quietly, if it ever gets easier. I told her the honest answer: it doesn't get easier, but you get bigger. More room in you for it. She teared up a little and then went to run a code. She ran it beautifully.

PGY-3 internal medicine

My patient this week was ninety-six and had outlived three doctors. When I introduced myself he said, oh good, another one. He held my hand through the whole exam and told me about his wife. When I left the room my attending said, that's the job. That's the whole job. I wrote it down.

The Consult

On what the job is.

Here is what almost twenty years inside medicine has taught me. Smart gets you in the door. Everyone here is smart. That is how you got here. What makes someone good at this job, year after year, is not intelligence. It is care. How much room you keep making. For the patient. For the team. For the family in the waiting room who no one has talked to in three hours. The people who do this work well get bigger on the inside until the job is just a shape they have grown around.

Right now there is a loud argument happening about whether AI is going to replace us. I have thoughts about that argument. The short version for today is this. AI is going to do a lot of what I do. It is going to read the scan faster than me. It is going to catch the drug interaction I miss. Good. My patients deserve that.

What it is not going to do is sit on the edge of a ninety-six-year-old man's bed and let him tell me about his wife. That is still our job. It might be the only job left that really is ours. And I think it is a beautiful one.

— Troy

Differential

Why does asparagus make your pee smell?

A classic. The culprit is asparagusic acid, a sulfur-containing compound unique to asparagus. Your body breaks it down into volatile sulfur compounds that exit through your urine within about fifteen minutes of eating. The interesting twist: not everyone can smell it. Roughly 40% of people have a genetic variant that blocks the olfactory receptors for those sulfur compounds. So some people produce the smell and can't detect it. Some people detect it but don't produce much. Some people do both.

Moral: asparagus is a genetic personality test disguised as a vegetable. See you next Sunday.
